The Pan American Health Organization says new regional agreements will make high-quality human papillomavirus (HPV) tests available to countries in Latin America and the Caribbean at more favorable prices. The agreements, announced November 11, 2026, are intended to address a practical obstacle in cervical cancer prevention: the cost and complexity of obtaining reliable tests.

PAHO says participating countries are expected to realize cost savings ranging from 30% to 70% through its Regional Revolving Funds. The arrangements also include implementation support, equipment access, training, maintenance and technical assistance. These are meaningful additions, because purchasing a test is only one step in a functioning screening programme.

The announcement does not mean that every woman in the region will immediately receive screening, or that national programmes can skip follow-up care. Lower prices can make access easier; health systems still need to organize testing, communicate results and ensure that people with positive results can obtain timely evaluation and treatment.

## Why HPV testing matters

Persistent infection with high-risk types of HPV causes nearly all cervical cancer cases. WHO says cervical cancer is largely preventable through HPV vaccination and regular screening in line with national guidance, and that it can be cured when detected early and treated promptly.

WHO’s fact sheet reports around 604,000 new cases and around 280,000 deaths worldwide in 2024. Those figures show the scale of the disease, but they do not describe the situation in every country equally. WHO notes that the highest incidence and mortality rates occur in settings with barriers to prevention, screening and care.

Screening looks for signs of risk before cancer develops or while disease is at an earlier stage. The method used and the recommended schedule depend on national guidelines and a person’s circumstances. This article is general information, not personal medical advice; people should discuss screening with a qualified health professional.

## What the agreements change

PAHO says countries and territories will be able to access molecular HPV tests through its Revolving Funds under harmonized regional conditions. The organization expects the new terms to lower costs by 30% to 70%, compared with prices countries have faced. “Expected” is important: the realized savings will depend on purchasing and implementation in each setting.

The agreement is not limited to a price list. PAHO describes a support package that includes equipment access, training, maintenance and technical assistance. These elements matter because testing requires reliable equipment, supplies, trained personnel, quality procedures and a way to deliver results to patients.

Unitaid supported the work and will assist implementation activities in El Salvador, Guatemala, Grenada, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, and Saint Lucia, according to PAHO. The organization says the support is intended to help introduce or expand HPV-based screening and strengthen follow-up for women with positive results.

PAHO reports that 16 countries and territories in Latin America and the Caribbean have introduced HPV testing in screening programmes, but coverage has been limited by cost. That gap illustrates why procurement matters—and why procurement alone cannot achieve high screening coverage.

## From a test to an effective programme

An effective screening pathway has several parts. People need to know where screening is offered and how to access it. Clinics need supplies and staff. Tests must be processed and results communicated. A positive result must lead to appropriate evaluation and care. If any link is missing, a lower-cost test may not translate into better outcomes.

PAHO’s announcement includes implementation support and training for this reason. Countries will still have to adapt the tools to their own health systems, budgets and service networks. Reaching rural and underserved communities may require different approaches from those used in major cities.

The agreements also fit into a broader goal. WHO’s global strategy sets 90-70-90 targets: 90% of girls fully vaccinated against HPV by age 15; 70% of women screened with a high-performance test by ages 35 and 45; and 90% of women with cervical disease treated. These are population targets, not a personal screening schedule. National recommendations should guide individual decisions.

## Why regional purchasing can help

Smaller health systems may have less bargaining power when purchasing diagnostics on their own. A regional mechanism can aggregate demand and establish shared terms, potentially making quality products more affordable and predictable. It can also help align technical support across countries.

But a pooled purchasing agreement is not the same as universal availability. Countries must decide how to use the mechanism, fund the services, integrate tests into existing programmes and track whether people receive follow-up. Supply and affordability are foundations; service delivery determines whether those foundations reach patients.

## What to watch next

The next indicators will be practical: which participating countries place orders, how quickly testing becomes available, whether the promised price reductions appear in actual procurement, and whether screening coverage and follow-up improve. Transparent reporting will help distinguish an agreement from its effects.

The announcement is a potentially important step because it tackles a cost barrier and bundles technical support with access to tests. Its public-health value will depend on the work that follows—national implementation, reliable services, clear information and timely care for patients who need it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do the agreements mean everyone will receive an HPV test immediately?

No. They establish regional access terms and support. Each country must implement screening through its health system.

### Does a positive HPV screening test mean someone has cancer?

No. Screening identifies risk and the need for follow-up; it is not itself a cancer diagnosis. A qualified health professional can explain the appropriate next steps under local guidance.

### Who should decide when an individual is screened?

People should follow their country’s screening guidance and discuss personal questions with a qualified healthcare professional.
